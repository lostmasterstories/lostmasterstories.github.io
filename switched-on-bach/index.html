Switched-On Bach

The story behind Switched-On Bach (1968), the album where Wendy Carlos played Bach on a Moog synthesizer, one note at a time. How a monophonic synthesizer, a homemade 8-track recorder and about a thousand hours of work made the first classical album ever certified platinum. A music history documentary with a source for every fact.

Bach on a synthesizer that played one note 0:00

In 1968, the Moog synthesizer had a strange limitation. It could play only one note at a time. No chords. No harmony. Just one single line. And yet, that same year, a record built entirely on that machine came out with twelve tracks of Johann Sebastian Bach. Music made of several voices moving at once. The album was called Switched-On Bach. It became the first classical album ever certified platinum. So how do you play Bach's counterpoint on a machine that can't play two notes together? This is the story of a thousand hours of work, a homemade tape machine, and an instrument that kept drifting out of tune.

The Moog synthesizer in the 1960s 0:40

In the mid-1960s, a synthesizer was not something you found in a music shop. Robert Moog's instruments were built by hand, in small numbers, and they were expensive. Moog later said his first market was experimental composers. And he remembered the mood around it bluntly: nobody believed there was any future in that sort of thing. A Moog was a cabinet of separate modules. Oscillators made the tone. Filters shaped it. Envelopes decided how each note began and faded. And you connected them with patch cables, by hand. But for anyone hoping to play real music on it, one fact mattered more than the rest. It was monophonic. One note at a time.

Who was Wendy Carlos 1:23

One of the people who heard real music in that machine was a young composer and engineer we know today as Wendy Carlos. Her first album came out under the name Walter Carlos. Carlos had studied electronic music at Columbia University, with Vladimir Ussachevsky and Otto Luening, at the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center. She didn't just buy a Moog. She worked with Bob Moog himself on a prototype touch-sensitive keyboard, so the instrument could respond to how a note was played. And she was able to get equipment at a discount. Her goal, in her own words, was to show that she could make real music with Bob Moog's marvelous new synthesizer. Not to revive Bach. To prove the instrument.

Why Bach? 2:07

So why Bach? Carlos has explained it. Bach's music is contrapuntal, built from clean, independent lines. It doesn't ask for big romantic expression. And it isn't tied to any one instrument. Of everything she had tried, Bach was the music most likely to turn into a record. The person who made it a record was producer Rachel Elkind. Benjamin Folkman assisted on the performances. Elkind, Folkman and Robert Moog himself all signed the liner notes.

Recording Switched-On Bach one line at a time 2:37

Here is the problem they faced. A Bach fugue can have three, four or more voices moving together. The Moog could produce one. So they recorded one voice at a time. Carlos would play a single line onto tape. Then go back and play the next line against it. To create a chord, as she put it, you'd play the second line, then the third. Layering like that needs a multitrack tape recorder. Carlos says she didn't have much money, so she built her own: an eight-track machine assembled from Ampex parts. And the instrument fought back. In Carlos's words, the Moog was very unstable and would go out of tune constantly. Play a phrase. Back up. Check. Retune. Continue. Sometimes a take held only a measure or two. All of it happened in a home studio in New York. In total, the project took about a thousand hours of work over five months. For roughly forty minutes of music.

The 1968 release and the wrong Bach cover 3:33

Columbia Masterworks released Switched-On Bach in 1968, catalogue number MS 7194. It didn't get a grand solo launch. Columbia put it out as part of a package with two other new records. Even the cover had a false start. The first pressing showed a seated Bach that collectors now call the Constipated Bach cover. It was pulled quickly and replaced with a standing Bach.

Billboard, three Grammys and the first platinum classical album 3:59

Then the record started selling. It climbed to number ten on the Billboard 200. Not the classical chart. The main album chart. On Billboard's classical chart it went to number one at the start of 1969, and stayed there for two years and forty-nine weeks. At the Grammy Awards in 1970 it won three: Classical Album of the Year, best classical performance by an instrumental soloist, and best engineered classical recording. By February 1974, Columbia estimated sales at nine hundred and sixty thousand copies. By June that year, it had passed one million. In 1986 the record industry certified it platinum. The first classical album ever to get there.

Legacy: Glenn Gould, Giorgio Moroder, A Clockwork Orange 4:46

The success changed how people saw the synthesizer. Robert Moog credited the album with breaking the idea that synthesizers were only good for sound effects and avant-garde music. He called it the birth of a new genre. Pianist Glenn Gould praised Carlos too. Of her later recording of the Fourth Brandenburg Concerto, he said it was the finest performance of any of the Brandenburgs, live, canned, or intuited, he had ever heard. Producer Giorgio Moroder has said this album is what drew his attention to synthesizers. And Carlos and Elkind went on to create the music for the film A Clockwork Orange.

Library of Congress and the answer 5:24

Decades later, the Library of Congress added Switched-On Bach to its National Recording Registry. So how did a machine that could play only one note at a time make the first classical album ever certified platinum? One line at a time. Recorded, checked, retuned, and stacked on a homemade tape machine, for about a thousand hours. Carlos said she just wanted to prove the instrument could make real music. More than a million listeners agreed.
